\section{Modelling framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Choice of the law}

The vessel imposes an oedometric strain path: the lateral strain is zero and the measured axial strain is a volumetric strain. As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:compressible}, the flow laws of dense ice are isochoric and cannot produce this deformation on their own. The law adopted here therefore has three parts:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item an \textbf{instantaneous skeleton term} for the rearrangement and sliding of the regolith grains during loading;
    \item a \textbf{compressible viscous term} of the porous type (Equation~\eqref{eq:porous}), which describes the closure of the pores;
    \item a \textbf{matrix law} for the ice inside the viscous term, taken from Goldsby and Kohlstedt (Equation~\eqref{eq:gk}).
\end{enumerate}
The total strain is
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon = \varepsilon_{sk}(\sigma) + \varepsilon_{cr}(t;\,\sigma, T, D, \phi_i),
    \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
where the skeleton term is written in the usual oedometric form $e = e_0 - C_c\log_{10}(\sigma/\sigma_{ref})$, calibrated on the dry test, and the viscous term follows Equation~\eqref{eq:porous} with
\begin{equation}
    B(T) = B\bigl(\dot\varepsilon_{GK}(T, d)\bigr), \qquad b = b(D, \phi_i).
\end{equation}
The ice fraction $\phi_i$ enters through $b$: the regolith grains change the geometry of the contacts and carry part of the load, so they act on the volumetric response rather than on the creep of the ice itself.

This choice has two advantages over a purely empirical power law with separate functions of the void ratio and of the ice fraction. First, $n$ and $Q$ are not free parameters but those of the ice, which the stress-step and temperature tests only have to verify; the stress-step test does not confirm the value of $n$, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:cal_stress}. Second, the law reduces to the known laws of ice and firn as $D\to1$ and $\phi_i\to1$, so that it can be compared with the firn data obtained on the same vessel~\cite{skarbek2025data}. The functions to be calibrated reduce essentially to $b(D,\phi_i)$, with $a(D)$ taken from the firn literature.

\subsection{Implications of the matrix law}

Two features of Equation~\eqref{eq:gk} affect the test programme and the use of the law.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Activation energy.} Above about $-15\,^\circ$C the activation energies of all mechanisms rise sharply. The test at $-10\,^\circ$C lies above this threshold and the others below, so $Q$ is fitted between $-30$ and $-20\,^\circ$C and the $-10\,^\circ$C test is treated separately.
    \item \textbf{Change of mechanism with stress.} At 2\,MPa and $-20\,^\circ$C, with grains of about 250\,$\mu$m, dislocation creep ($n\approx4$) dominates. At the stresses of a lunar foundation (0.1--0.2\,MPa) grain boundary sliding ($n\approx1.8$) takes over. Extrapolating the laboratory data to 0.2\,MPa with $n = 4$ would underestimate the creep rate by a factor of about 4--8 for these grain sizes. The composite law predicts that the apparent stress exponent measured in the stress-step test increases with stress between 0.5 and 3.4\,MPa, from about 2 to 4. The exponent measured in F216 is higher than both (Section~\ref{subsec:cal_stress}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}
\subsection{Stress dependence}
\label{subsec:cal_stress}

The stress-step test (Section~\ref{subsec:stress}) gives three results: $C_\alpha$ is the same at all stresses, the void ratio at equal time is linear in $\log\sigma$, and the loading path has little effect on the void ratio reached. These are the properties of the isotache models of soil mechanics~\cite{bjerrum1967,mesri1977}, in which the rate of compaction is a unique function of stress and void ratio. Under constant stress
\begin{equation}
    e = e_{ref} - C_c\log_{10}\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{ref}} - C_\alpha\log_{10}\frac{t}{\tau_{ref}},
    \label{eq:isotache_t}
\end{equation}
and, eliminating time,
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\mathrm{d}e}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{C_\alpha}{\tau_{ref}\ln10}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{ref}}\right)^{C_c/C_\alpha}\exp\!\left[\frac{\ln10}{C_\alpha}\,(e - e_{ref})\right].
    \label{eq:isotache}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache} has the form of a creep law: a power of the stress, with exponent $n = C_c/C_\alpha$, times an exponential function of the void ratio. With the values of F216 for the mixture with $\phi_i = 0.50$ at $-20\,^\circ$C, $C_c = 0.31$, $C_\alpha = 0.045$ and $e_{ref} = 0.497$ at $\sigma_{ref} = 1$\,MPa and $\tau_{ref} = 10.8$\,h, the exponent is $n\approx7$, consistent with the values of 5--7 obtained directly from the rates at equal void ratio. For direct loading to 2\,MPa Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache_t} gives $e = 0.404$ at 10.8\,h, against 0.429 measured in F213; the difference is of the order of the uncertainty on the initial state of the two samples.

Two points follow for the law of Section~\ref{sec:framework}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The stress exponent is not that of the ice.} The mechanisms of Equation~\eqref{eq:gk} have $n$ between 1.8 and 4. An exponent of about 7 in terms of nominal stress, and a compression index equal to that of the dry regolith, indicate that the dependence on stress is controlled by the regolith skeleton, or that the ice at the contacts works at stresses far above the nominal one, where the power law no longer holds. The two readings cannot be separated with one test; stress-step tests at other ice contents, where the role of the skeleton changes, are the most direct check.
    \item \textbf{The ratio $C_\alpha/C_c$ is high.} It is about 0.15, against 0.025--0.10 for natural soils~\cite{mesri1977}. In the dry regolith it is about 0.005. The ice leaves the compressibility of the skeleton almost unchanged and makes it strongly dependent on time.
\end{itemize}
The stress used here is the oil pressure. The seal friction (about 170\,kPa in F216) and the wall friction reduce the stress on the sample, mostly in the first step; correcting for the seal friction lowers the exponent by about 1.

\subsection{Temperature dependence}
% F215 (-40 C) is excluded.
The first results of the temperature series (Section~\ref{subsec:temperature}) show three features that the calibration must reproduce. $C_\alpha$ is the same at $-30$ and $-20\,^\circ$C, so temperature enters the isotache law of Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache} only through the reference time $\tau_{ref}$, as a shift of the curves along the time axis. The temperature-step test F219 confirms this on a single sample: after warming from $-30$ to $-20\,^\circ$C the curve is again linear in log time, once the time is counted from an equivalent age ten times shorter. Its slope increases somewhat at each warming; the calibration of Section~\ref{subsec:evp1d} quantifies this increase at about 20\% per 10\,K. The same holds from $-20$ to $-10\,^\circ$C. The shift is a factor of about 10 from $-30$ to $-20\,^\circ$C and 13--14 from $-20$ to $-10\,^\circ$C in F219 (apparent activation energy about 120 and 140--145\,kJ/mol), against 3--5 between F218 and F213 (55--85\,kJ/mol); the single-sample values are the more reliable, and are higher than the activation energies of ice below $-15\,^\circ$C, as expected if the unfrozen water at the ice--mineral contacts contributes. In F212 at $-10\,^\circ$C the curve bent downwards in $\log t$, but F219 does not reproduce this, so it is attributed to that test. The increase of the activation energy towards the melting point means that a single Arrhenius factor between $-30$ and $-10\,^\circ$C is not exact; the matrix law of Goldsby and Kohlstedt, with its higher activation energies above about $-15\,^\circ$C, provides the natural form for this change.

Since the rate at equal void ratio depends on stress and temperature through $\sigma^{n}\exp(-Q/RT)$, a change of temperature that multiplies the rate by a factor $F$ has the same effect on creep as multiplying the stress by $F^{1/n}$. With $F\approx10$--14 and $n\approx5.5$--7, a warming of 10\,K is equivalent, for the creep rate, to raising the stress by 40--60\%. The equivalence does not extend to the immediate response: an increase of stress also compresses the regolith skeleton at once, by $C_c\log(\sigma_2/\sigma_1)$, while an increase of temperature acts only on the ice, through the reference time $\tau_{ref}$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache}, and produces no immediate compression. It holds only where temperature does not change the shape of the creep curve, which F219 confirms between $-30$ and $-10\,^\circ$C.
\subsection{Dependence on porosity}
\label{subsec:cal_porosity}

\paragraph{The porous law in the oedometer.} Setting the lateral strain rate to zero in Equation~\eqref{eq:porous} gives the deviatoric stress $q = \sigma_z - \sigma_r = 2\,b\,p/a$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
    K_0 = \frac{\sigma_r}{\sigma_z} = \frac{1 - \tfrac{2}{3}\,b/a}{1 + \tfrac{4}{3}\,b/a}, \qquad
    \dot\varepsilon_z = B\,\sigma_z^{\,n}\,\frac{b^{(n+1)/2}}{\left(1 + \tfrac{4}{3}\,b/a\right)^{(n+1)/2}}.
    \label{eq:porous_oed}
\end{equation}
The axial strain rate depends on the density through $b^{(n+1)/2}$, that is, through $b^2$ for $n = 3$. With the exponential form of Equation~\eqref{eq:ab_gm} the rate is then close to an exponential function of the void ratio, as in Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache}. A law of this kind has no steady state in the oedometer: the rate decreases as the pores close, and an exponential dependence on $e$ integrates to a straight line in $\log t$ with
\begin{equation}
    C_\alpha \approx \frac{\ln10}{\kappa}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\partial\ln\dot\varepsilon_z}{\partial e}.
    \label{eq:calpha_kappa}
\end{equation}
The log-linear creep observed in the tests is therefore what a porous law predicts, and $C_\alpha$ measures how strongly the rate depends on the void ratio.

The fluidity is that of Glen's law with $n = 3$, multiplied by the ratio of about 14 between the laws of Goldsby and Kohlstedt and of Glen at 2\,MPa.

\subsection{Dependence on ice content}
\label{subsec:cal_ice}

\paragraph{Structure of the mixtures.} The void ratio of the soil skeleton alone, $e_s$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:Si}, counts both the ice and the gas as pore space: $e_s = (e+\phi_i)/(1-\phi_i)$, with $e$ referred to all the solids. Table~\ref{tab:es} gives its value at $e = 0.45$ and at the end of the tests. The loosest state reached by the dry LHS-1 in this vessel is $e_s = 1.07$ (F208, poured). For $\phi_i\ge0.38$ the skeleton void ratio is above this value throughout the tests: the regolith grains alone cannot form a continuous load-bearing skeleton, and the load is carried by an assembly of regolith and ice grains. This is possible because the ice grains (about 250\,$\mu$m) are four times coarser than the median regolith grain (60\,$\mu$m, Section~\ref{subsec:mixtures}): they are not a pore filler but part of the granular structure, with the fines between them. At $\phi_i = 0.75$ the regolith is dispersed in the ice ($e_s\approx4$--5), the material is ice with particles, and F209 indeed behaves as firn.

\paragraph{Two limiting views.} The data at equal void ratio (Table~\ref{tab:equal_e}) can be compared with the porous law for firn in two ways. In the first, the mixture is treated as a porous ice whose void ratio is that of all the solids; this is the comparison of Table~\ref{tab:firn_comparison}, which gives the mixtures with $\phi_i\le0.50$ 100--250 times slower than firn. In the second, the regolith is an inert filler and the ice carries all the load; the relevant void ratio is then that of the ice alone, $e/\phi_i$, which at $e = 0.45$ is 0.60, 0.90 and 1.18 for $\phi_i = 0.75$, 0.50 and 0.38. Equation~\eqref{eq:porous_oed} at these void ratios predicts rates higher than those measured by a factor of about 12 for $\phi_i = 0.75$, and by factors of $10^5$--$10^7$ for $\phi_i\le0.50$; the last values extrapolate the firn law beyond its range of calibration, but the conclusion does not depend on their precision. The mixtures with half of ice or less are therefore far from the inert-filler view and much closer to the first one: the regolith grains carry part of the load and slow down the creep of the ice. This is the same conclusion reached from the stress exponent (Section~\ref{subsec:cal_stress}).

\paragraph{A first calibration of $b(D,\phi_i)$.} Keeping the form of Equation~\eqref{eq:ab_gm}, the function $b$ is written as
\begin{equation}
    b(D,\phi_i) = \exp\bigl[-\zeta(\phi_i)\,D + c(\phi_i)\bigr],
    \label{eq:b_phi}
\end{equation}
with $a(D)$ unchanged. For $n = 3$ the axial rate in Equation~\eqref{eq:porous_oed} varies approximately as $b^2$, the denominator changing slowly with density, so that Equation~\eqref{eq:calpha_kappa} gives
\begin{equation}
    \zeta \approx \frac{\ln10\,(1+e)^2}{2\,C_\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The exponent $\zeta$ is fixed by $C_\alpha$, and $c$ by the rate at a reference void ratio (Table~\ref{tab:b_calibration}). For $\phi_i = 0.75$ the value obtained, $\zeta\approx24$, is close to the 27.65 of firn; for $\phi_i\le0.50$ it is about twice as large, which expresses the faster decrease of the rate as the pores close. The factor $g$ in the table is the ratio of the measured rate to that of firn at the same void ratio.

\subsection{Parameter uncertainty}
\label{subsec:uncertainty}

The main sources of uncertainty on the parameters, and their effect, are the following.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seal and wall friction.} The stresses are oil pressures. Subtracting the seal friction of about 170\,kPa lowers the stress exponent from 6--7.5 to 5.3--5.7, mostly through the first step at 0.5\,MPa; the wall friction, not measured, acts in the same direction. $C_\alpha$ and the temperature factors are not affected, since they are obtained at constant stress.
    \item \textbf{Scale of the displacement measurement.} The LVDT scale varies between tests (0.86--1.19). Anchoring each test to the measured initial and final heights fixes the void ratios at the two ends; the error on $C_\alpha$ is proportional to that on the scale, about 10--15\% where the scale is uncertain (F219). Ratios of rates at equal void ratio, and therefore the temperature factors and $Q$, do not depend on the scale.
    \item \textbf{Initial height.} An error of 0.5\,mm on the height changes the void ratio by about 0.02 over the whole test, but not $C_\alpha$ or the rate factors.
    \item \textbf{Scatter between samples.} Two tests under the same conditions agree to 0.01 in void ratio (F213 and F214), but F219 crept much faster than F218 and F213. Comparisons between samples are therefore uncertain by more than the effect of 10\,K, which is why the single-sample value of $Q$ (120--145\,kJ/mol) is preferred to the value from separate samples (55--85\,kJ/mol).
    \item \textbf{Temperature.} The temperature of the sample is not recorded and is assumed equal to the set point of the bath once it is stable; the first 2\,h after each change are excluded.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Flow laws of ice.} At 100\,min the strain rates of the mixtures are of the order of $10^{-6}$\,s$^{-1}$, as predicted for dense ice at 2\,MPa and $-20\,^\circ$C, but the dependence on stress and temperature is stronger than that of any mechanism of Glen's or of Goldsby and Kohlstedt's law. The laws of ice describe the matrix; they do not describe the mixture on their own, both because they are isochoric and because the regolith takes part in the load transfer (Section~\ref{subsec:cal_ice}).

\paragraph{Firn densification.} The porous law reproduces the form of the response (log-linear creep without steady state) and, for $\phi_i = 0.75$, also its magnitude. For smaller ice contents it must be modified through $b(D,\phi_i)$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:b_phi}).

\paragraph{Creep laws of frozen soils.} The engineering laws of Ladanyi type write the creep strain as a power function of stress and time, that is, they are time-hardening. F219 allows a direct comparison with the isotache form, which is density-hardening. After a change of temperature at time $t_1$, a time-hardening law gives a rate decreasing as $1/t = 1/(t_1+\Delta t)$, while the isotache law gives $1/(t_{eq}+\Delta t)$ with $t_{eq} = t_1/F$. Between 2 and 49\,h after the change from $-30$ to $-20\,^\circ$C ($t_1 = 3943$\,min) the time-hardening law predicts a decrease of the rate by a factor of $(3943+2950)/(3943+120)\approx1.7$; the isotache law predicts $(380+2950)/(380+120)\approx6.7$; the measured decrease is about 6. The same holds after the change to $-10\,^\circ$C. The isotache form, with the void ratio as state variable, is therefore required to follow changes of temperature, and, as the stress-step test shows, changes of stress. Models of the EVPFROZEN family~\cite{cudmani2022} have the right structure for temperature and stress, but would need the void ratio as a state variable to describe the densification of icy regolith.

\paragraph{Isotache models of soils.} The mixtures follow the isotache framework of soil mechanics, with an unusually high ratio $C_\alpha/C_c$ and with a reference time that depends strongly on temperature. A one-dimensional elastic--viscoplastic model of the Yin--Graham type~\cite{yin1989}, with an Arrhenius reference time, therefore describes the oedometric tests directly, and its three-dimensional generalisations offer a route to numerical implementation alternative to the porous law of ice.

\section{Elastic--viscoplastic model}
\label{sec:implementation}

The isotache law of Section~\ref{sec:calibration} was turned into an elastic--viscoplastic model in three steps of increasing generality: a one-dimensional model, integrated along the measured load and temperature histories and calibrated on all the tests (Section~\ref{subsec:evp1d}); its extension to a general stress state as a Drucker--Prager/cap model with Perzyna-type viscoplasticity (Section~\ref{subsec:evp3d}); and a finite element model of the sample in the vessel (Section~\ref{subsec:fe_sample}). The three were written in MATLAB. The commercial code Abaqus was also used, with its built-in cap creep model, to check what a standard tool can and cannot reproduce (Section~\ref{subsec:abaqus}). The MATLAB scripts and the Abaqus input files are kept with the data of the tests.

\subsection{Viscoplastic framework}
\label{subsec:evp_framework}

Two families of models describe time-dependent soils~\cite{perzyna1966,yin1989,vermeer1999}. In \emph{overstress} models, of the Perzyna type, the viscoplastic strain rate is a function of the distance between the current stress and a static yield surface; inside the surface the response is elastic. In \emph{creep} models the viscoplastic strain rate is a function of stress and of a hardening variable, without a separate static surface. The isotache law of Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache} belongs to both: it can be read as a creep law in which the void ratio is the hardening variable, or as a Perzyna law with an exponential overstress function, in which the reference surface is the isochrone of age $\tau_{ref}$ and the overstress is measured by the distance of the void ratio from it~\cite{yin1989}. Simo et al.\ formulated the cap model of soil plasticity with a Perzyna-type rate dependence~\cite{simo1988}; the model of this section follows the same idea, with the exponential function of the isotache law in place of the power function of the original Perzyna law. The choice of the exponential is dictated by the tests: only an exponential function of the void ratio gives a creep that is linear in $\log t$ and continues without a steady state, as observed.

\subsection{One-dimensional model and calibration}
\label{subsec:evp1d}

In one dimension the model reads
\begin{equation}
    \dot e = -\frac{C_s}{\ln10}\,\frac{\dot\sigma}{\sigma}
    \;-\; \frac{C_\alpha}{\ln10\;\tau_0}\;a_T(T)\;10^{\,[e - e_{NC}(\sigma)]/C_\alpha},
    \qquad e_{NC}(\sigma) = e_{ref} - C_c\log_{10}\sigma,
    \label{eq:evp1d}
\end{equation}
where the first term is the elastic response of the skeleton, $e_{NC}$ is the reference isochrone of age $\tau_0 = 1$\,min, and the temperature acts only through the time-shift factor
\begin{equation}
    a_T(T) = \exp\!\left[-\frac{Q}{R_g}\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\right)\right], \qquad T_{ref} = 253.15\,\text{K}.
    \label{eq:aT}
\end{equation}
At constant stress and temperature Equation~\eqref{eq:evp1d} integrates to Equation~\eqref{eq:isotache_t}. After a change of stress or temperature no restart is needed: the memory of the previous history is carried entirely by the void ratio, and the rate resumes from the equivalent age of the sample (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}). Equation~\eqref{eq:evp1d} was integrated with an implicit solver (\texttt{ode15s}) along the measured oil pressure of each test (median-filtered over nine readings) and along the history of the bath set point, starting from the measured void ratio when the load reached 1.9\,MPa and ending at the start of unloading.

\paragraph{Calibration.} The parameters were fitted by least squares on 120 points per test, evenly spaced in $\log t$. $C_c$ and $C_s$ are common to all the tests; $C_\alpha$ and $e_{ref}$ are fitted for each test, so that $e_{ref}$ absorbs the differences in initial state between samples. The activation energy is not fitted but fixed at $Q = 130$\,kJ/mol, within the range of 120--145\,kJ/mol obtained from the temperature steps of F219 (Section~\ref{subsec:temperature}). Table~\ref{tab:evp1d} gives the parameters and Figure~\ref{fig:evp1d} the comparison with the tests.

Four results follow.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The compression index fitted on the creep data is that of the stress-step test.} $C_c = 0.313$, obtained from the whole set of curves, coincides with the value of 0.31 obtained in Section~\ref{subsec:cal_stress} from the void ratios at equal time. The model reproduces the immediate compression and the renewed creep after the stress step of F216 without any parameter specific to the step.
    \item \textbf{The model follows the temperature steps without restart.} In F219 the rate after each warming is reproduced with the time-shift factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:aT} alone, which confirms the density-hardening form of the law.
    \item \textbf{$C_\alpha$ increases with temperature in F219.} With $Q = 130$\,kJ/mol and a constant $C_\alpha$ the model misses the last two stages of F219 by up to 0.07 in void ratio. Leaving $Q$ free, the fit requires $Q\approx220$\,kJ/mol, well above the value measured directly at the steps, and still leaves a maximum error of 0.018. With $Q$ fixed and $C_\alpha$ increasing by about 20\% per 10\,K the maximum error falls to 0.009. Warming the mixture therefore not only accelerates the creep but also increases its magnitude per decade of time. F213 and F218, at $-20$ and $-30\,^\circ$C, have the same $C_\alpha$, so this effect is seen only on the single sample of F219 and should be confirmed.
    \item \textbf{The scatter between samples is carried by $e_{ref}$.} With a single $e_{ref}$ for all the tests the rms error rises to about 0.011. The differences between samples of the same mixture are of the same order as the effect of 10\,K (Section~\ref{subsec:uncertainty}), so the temperature dependence is constrained by F219 and not by the comparison between F213 and F218.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three-dimensional formulation}
\label{subsec:evp3d}

\paragraph{Yield surfaces.} The extension to a general stress state uses the two surfaces of the Drucker--Prager/cap model (Figure~\ref{fig:evp3d}). The failure surface is the Drucker--Prager cone of Equation~\eqref{eq:dp_shear}, written as
\begin{equation}
    F_s = q - M\,(p + p_t) = 0, \qquad M = \frac{6\sin\varphi}{3-\sin\varphi}, \qquad p_t = c\,\cot\varphi,
    \label{eq:dp_shear}
\end{equation}
with $p$ the mean stress, $q$ the Mises equivalent stress, $\varphi$ and $c$ the Mohr--Coulomb friction angle and cohesion, $M$ the slope of the cone in triaxial compression and $p_t$ the isotropic tensile strength. In compression, the state is described by an elliptical cap through the current stress point,
\begin{equation}
    p_{eq} = p' + \frac{q^2}{M_c^2\,p'}, \qquad p' = p + p_t,
    \label{eq:peq}
\end{equation}
where $p_{eq}$ is the equivalent pressure, the intercept of the ellipse with the hydrostatic axis, and $M_c$ its shape parameter.

\paragraph{Viscoplastic strain rate on the cap.} The volumetric viscoplastic strain rate is the one-dimensional law written in terms of $p_{eq}$:
\begin{equation}
    \dot\varepsilon_v^{vp} = \frac{C_\alpha}{(1+e)\ln10\;\tau_0}\;a_T(T)\;10^{\,[e - e_{ref} + C_c\log_{10}(p_{eq}/k_{NC})]/C_\alpha},
    \label{eq:evp3d}
\end{equation}
and the strain rate tensor follows the normal to the cap,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}^{vp} = \frac{\dot\varepsilon_v^{vp}}{(\partial p_{eq}/\partial p)_{K_0}}\;\frac{\partial p_{eq}}{\partial\boldsymbol\sigma}.
    \label{eq:evp3d_flow}
\end{equation}
The constant $k_{NC} = p_{eq}/\sigma_z$ on the normally consolidated oedometric path makes Equation~\eqref{eq:evp3d} coincide with Equation~\eqref{eq:evp1d} in the oedometer, so that the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:evp1d} are used unchanged. The normalisation by the value of $\partial p_{eq}/\partial p$ on the same path follows the Soft Soil Creep model~\cite{vermeer1999}; it keeps the rate finite when the stress ratio exceeds the top of the cap ($q/p' > M_c$). For zero lateral strain the viscoplastic strain must be purely axial, which fixes the shape of the cap from the coefficient of earth pressure at rest:
\begin{equation}
    M_c^2 = \eta_0^2 + 3\,\eta_0, \qquad \eta_0 = \frac{3\,(1-K_0)}{1+2K_0}.
    \label{eq:Mc_K0}
\end{equation}
This is the counterpart of Equation~\eqref{eq:porous_oed} for the porous law: $K_0$ is an input of the model, not a result.

\paragraph{Viscoplastic strain rate on the cone.} Above the failure surface a deviatoric, isochoric strain rate is added with a Perzyna power law,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}^{vp}_s = \frac{a_T(T)}{\tau_s}\left\langle\frac{F_s}{M\,(p+p_t)}\right\rangle^{N}\frac{\partial q}{\partial\boldsymbol\sigma},
    \label{eq:perzyna_cone}
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ are Macaulay brackets. No test reached the failure surface, so $\tau_s = 1$\,min and $N = 3$ are assumptions; they control how fast the material fails once the cone is reached, not the creep below it.

\paragraph{Elasticity.} The elastic response is hypoelastic, with the constrained modulus of the one-dimensional law, $E_{oed} = (1+e)\ln10\,\sigma_z/C_s$ with $\sigma_z = p/[(1+2K_0)/3]$, and a Poisson ratio $\nu = 0.3$.

\paragraph{Strength parameters.} The oedometric tests do not involve the failure surface, so $\varphi$ and $c$ were taken from the literature. Dense dry LHS-1 ($\rho_d = 1.85$\,g/cm$^3$, relative density 86\%) gave $\varphi = 45.1^\circ$ at peak and $40.3^\circ$ at large strain in direct shear, with a cohesion below 2\,kPa~\cite{yin2023shear}. In sand saturated with ice the friction angle is not higher than in the unfrozen sand (31.4$^\circ$ against 37$^\circ$ at $-12\,^\circ$C~\cite{alkire1973}), and the ice acts mainly on the cohesion, which reaches several MPa in ice-cemented sand at $-10\,^\circ$C~\cite{parameswaran1981}. The mixtures of this work are not cemented: the ice is in grains of the same size as the coarse regolith grains and is compacted, not frozen in place. The unconfined strengths of 50--231\,kPa measured at $-20\,^\circ$C on a highland simulant with 3--12\% of water~\cite{pitcher2016} correspond, with $\varphi = 40^\circ$, to cohesions of 12--54\,kPa. The reference values adopted are $\varphi = 40^\circ$ and $c = 30$\,kPa, with sensitivity analyses over $\varphi = 35$--45$^\circ$ and $c = 10$--60\,kPa.

\paragraph{Coefficient of earth pressure at rest.} $K_0$ was not measured. Jaky's relation, $K_0 = 1-\sin\varphi$, gives $K_0 = 0.35$ for $\varphi = 40^\circ$; the porous law and firn give about 0.5--0.6 (Section~\ref{subsec:cal_porosity}), and a creeping ice matrix tends towards isotropic stress. $K_0 = 0.35$ is used as reference. A higher value is not compatible with $\varphi = 40^\circ$ in this model: with $K_0 = 0.6$, Equation~\eqref{eq:Mc_K0} gives $M_c = 1.38$, lower than the slope of the cone ($M = 1.64$), so the cap rather than the cone limits the strength, and a triaxial load at 80\% of the Drucker--Prager strength fails by accelerating creep within minutes. $K_0$ and $\varphi$ must therefore be chosen together; $K_0 = 0.6$ would correspond to an effective friction angle of about $34^\circ$.

\paragraph{Single-point checks.} The model was integrated for a material point under two kinds of control (Figure~\ref{fig:evp3d}): oedometric (axial stress prescribed, lateral strain zero) and drained triaxial (axial and radial stresses prescribed).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oedometer.} Along the measured history of F213 the three-dimensional model reproduces the one-dimensional one within 0.003--0.008 in void ratio for all the combinations of $c = 10$--60\,kPa, $\varphi = 35$--45$^\circ$ and $K_0 = 0.35$ or 0.6, and the ratio $\sigma_r/\sigma_z$ stays at the $K_0$ imposed through $M_c$. The small differences come from the shift $p_t$ of the mean stress, which Equation~\eqref{eq:evp3d} does not compensate. The oedometric tests therefore cannot determine $c$, $\varphi$ or $K_0$.
    \item \textbf{Triaxial creep.} After one day of oedometric creep at 2\,MPa and $-20\,^\circ$C, the axial stress was raised in 1\,min at constant radial stress to 80\% of the Drucker--Prager failure value and held for one day. The axial strain after one day is 3.0\%, 6.6\% and 14.1\% for $\varphi = 35$, 40 and 45$^\circ$ (with $c = 30$\,kPa), and 6.1\%, 6.6\% and 7.4\% for $c = 10$, 30 and 60\,kPa (with $\varphi = 40^\circ$). A higher friction angle allows a higher deviator stress at the same fraction of the strength, which raises the equivalent pressure of Equation~\eqref{eq:peq} and with it the creep on the cap: most of this strain is volumetric compaction driven by shear, not flow on the cone.
\end{itemize}

The strength parameters, irrelevant for the tests of this work, control the response as soon as the load has a significant deviatoric part, as under the edge of a foundation: within the range of the literature, $\varphi$ changes the predicted creep by a factor of about five and $c$ by about 20\%. Triaxial tests on the mixtures, or at least a measurement of the radial stress in the vessel, are needed before the model is used in such problems.

\subsection{Finite element model of the sample}
\label{subsec:fe_sample}

In an ideal oedometer the stress is uniform and a material point is enough. In the vessel the sample is tall (height-to-diameter ratio of 1.6--1.8) and slides along the steel wall as it compacts, so that friction on the wall can carry part of the load. To estimate this effect, the model of Section~\ref{subsec:evp3d} was implemented in an axisymmetric finite element code written in MATLAB (Figure~\ref{fig:fe_friction}c).

\paragraph{Model.} The sample of F213 is discretised with $6\times18$ four-node axisymmetric elements with $2\times2$ Gauss points, with a radius of 12.7\,mm and a height of 39.8\,mm at the start of the creep stage. The boundary conditions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a rigid piston on top: one common vertical displacement for all the top nodes, loaded by the measured oil pressure times the piston area;
    \item rough frits at the top and bottom: zero radial displacement, and zero vertical displacement at the bottom;
    \item a rigid side wall with Coulomb friction, $\tau = \mu\,\sigma_r$, opposing the downward sliding of the sample.
\end{itemize}
At each time step the viscoplastic strain increment is computed explicitly from the stress at the start of the step, and the displacements from an elastic solution with the viscoplastic increment as an initial strain, with an equilibrium correction (initial-strain method). The time step is adapted so that the viscoplastic strain increment does not exceed $2\times10^{-4}$; a simulation of F213 (66\,h) takes about 700 steps and 12\,s. The model starts at the beginning of the creep stage from a uniform state at the measured void ratio, so that the wall friction develops only during creep, and small strains are assumed.

\paragraph{Verification.} Without wall friction the finite element model gives a final void ratio of 0.3919, against 0.3921 for the material point and 0.3907 measured, with uniform stress and void ratio in the sample.

Second, the wall friction reduces the stress most at the lowest loads, in the same direction as the seal friction (Section~\ref{subsec:uncertainty}). It can be reduced with shorter samples (height-to-diameter ratio of about 0.5, as in standard oedometers) or a lubricated wall, and estimated from the profile of density measured by slicing a sample after the test.

\subsection{Implementation in a commercial code}
\label{subsec:abaqus}

The modified Drucker--Prager/cap model of Abaqus/Standard~\cite{abaqus2026} has the same structure as the model of Section~\ref{subsec:evp3d}: a Drucker--Prager surface, an elliptical cap that hardens with the volumetric inelastic strain, and a \emph{consolidation creep} mechanism that produces volumetric creep under the cap. It was used to check whether the behaviour measured in the tests can be reproduced without writing a user material. The tests were simulated with one axisymmetric element (CAX4, radius 12.7\,mm, height 45\,mm) with the boundary conditions of an ideal oedometer, the measured load history and the bath temperature as a field. The cap was given $\beta = 57.6^\circ$ ($\varphi = 38.6^\circ$~\cite{agarwal2023}), $d = 0.01$\,MPa, $R = 1.11$ (from $K_0 = 0.6$), $E = 300$\,MPa and $\nu = 0.3$, and the hardening law of the cap was tabulated from the normal compression line with $C_c = 0.31$.

\paragraph{Constraints of the Learning Edition.} User subroutines are not available in the Learning Edition, nor with the teaching licence of Politecnico di Milano, and no Fortran compiler was available, so the isotache law could not be introduced through the subroutine \texttt{CREEP}. Of the built-in laws, the strain-hardening and power laws, which would approximate the density hardening of the isotache law, could not be used: their rate is infinite at zero creep strain, and the analysis stopped as soon as the cap was reached, with time increments of $5\times10^{-8}$\,s. Only the time-hardening law,
\begin{equation}
    \dot\varepsilon^{cr} = A(T)\,(p^{cr})^{n}\,t^{m},
    \label{eq:abq_timehard}
\end{equation}
could be used. With $n = 0.35$ and $m = -0.95$, close to a logarithmic creep, and $A$ calibrated on F213, the model reproduces the tests at constant load and temperature (F213, and F218 with the same $A$) within 0.03 in void ratio in the first minutes and 0.006 after one day (Figure~\ref{fig:abaqus}a).

\paragraph{Limit of the consolidation creep.} The tests with steps of stress or temperature are not reproduced (Figure~\ref{fig:abaqus}b, c). After the stress step of F216 the model compacts by 0.02 in void ratio, against 0.07 measured, and in F219 the creep stops before the first change of temperature, so that warming has no effect. The reason is structural. In the consolidation creep mechanism the creep strain also hardens the cap, and the driving stress is $p^{cr} = p - p_a$: as the material compacts, $p_a$ approaches $p$ and the creep stops, whatever the creep law. The maximum creep strain at constant stress follows from the hardening law and the shape of the cap,
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon^{cr}_{max} \approx \frac{C_c}{1+e_0}\,\log_{10}(1 + R\tan\beta) \approx 0.075,
    \label{eq:abq_ceiling}
\end{equation}
in agreement with the 0.064 reached in F213 after 2.7 days and the 0.085 of F219. In the tests the creep continues well beyond this value: after the first hour F219 compacted by a further 0.36 in void ratio, about 0.18 in axial strain. The consolidation creep of Abaqus is therefore a bounded, delayed compression, not an isotache creep.

\paragraph{Stage-wise analysis with equivalent age.} The tests with steps can be followed by treating each stage as a separate analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:abaqus_stages}). Each analysis starts from a normally consolidated state at the measured void ratio at the beginning of the stage; the creep, with the time-hardening law written in total time, starts at the equivalent age $\tau$ given by the isotache law, reached through a static step in which creep is inactive. The equivalent ages are 394\,min after the change from $-30$ to $-20\,^\circ$C (3944\,min divided by the factor of 10), 246\,min after the change to $-10\,^\circ$C, and 21\,min after the stress step of F216 ($648\,\text{min}\times(2/3.4)^{6.5}$). These values are not fitted: free fits of each stage of F219 with a logarithmic law give 333 and 241\,min. With a single rescaling of $A$ per stage (by 2.4 and 2.7 in F219, 1.23 in F216) the analyses reproduce the shape of the curves after each step within 0.01 in void ratio. The first stage of F219 exceeds the limit of Equation~\eqref{eq:abq_ceiling} and was split into three analyses (1\,min--1\,h, 1--10\,h, 10--66\,h) with the same $A$, which end within 0.003--0.016 of the measured void ratio.

The stage-wise procedure shows that the equivalent age of the isotache law is the right way to carry the history across a change of stress or temperature. It remains a workaround: the need to rescale $A$ reflects the increase of $C_\alpha$ in F219 seen in Section~\ref{subsec:evp1d}, and the splitting of a stage restores the capacity of the cap to creep by an artifice, not by physics. For boundary value problems the model of Section~\ref{subsec:evp3d} should be implemented as a user material (\texttt{UMAT}) in a full licence of Abaqus, or in another finite element code, rather than approximated with the built-in creep laws.

\section{Limitations and range of validity}
\label{sec:model_limits}

The parameters of this chapter are valid within the ranges covered by the tests (Table~\ref{tab:validity}). Outside them the law is an extrapolation, and three extrapolations matter most for lunar applications: to lower stresses, to lower temperatures and to longer times.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Variable & Range tested \\
\midrule
Ice content & $w = 0$--1.0 ($\phi_i = 0$--0.75); stress dependence only at $w = 0.33$ \\
Temperature & $-30$ to $-10\,^\circ$C \\
Axial stress (oil pressure) & 0.5--3.4\,MPa \\
Void ratio & 1.07 to 0.08 \\
Duration & up to 161\,h (one test); 40--65\,h typical \\
Pore fluid & air at atmospheric pressure \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Range covered by the tests.}
\label{tab:validity}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stress.} A lunar foundation applies 0.1--0.2\,MPa, below the range tested and close to the seal friction of the vessel (about 0.17\,MPa), which therefore cannot measure in this range. With the measured exponent of 5.5--7.5 the creep at 0.2\,MPa would be very slow; if grain boundary sliding ($n\approx1.8$) takes over at low stress, it would be much faster. The more conservative extrapolation should be used.
    \item \textbf{Temperature.} The lunar polar regolith is at 40--100\,K. The activation energy measured between $-30$ and $-10\,^\circ$C, and its increase towards the melting point, cannot be extrapolated with confidence over more than 150\,K; at these temperatures the creep is in any case negligible, and the law is needed only where a structure warms the ground.
    \item \textbf{Time.} The tests last at most a week, while a structure is designed for years. Extrapolating the log-linear creep over three or four decades of time assumes that no new mechanism (pore close-off, gas back-pressure, change of $C_\alpha$) appears.
    \item \textbf{State of stress.} The radial stress is not measured: $K_0$, the friction angle and the cohesion come from the literature, and they control the response of the three-dimensional model as soon as the load has a deviatoric part (Section~\ref{subsec:evp3d}).
    \item \textbf{Wall friction.} The parameters assume a uniform stress in the sample. The finite element model shows that a wall friction coefficient of 0.2 would reduce the stress at the bottom of the sample by about a quarter and the mean compaction by 0.03 in void ratio (Section~\ref{subsec:fe_sample}); the parameters are therefore apparent values until the wall friction is measured.
    \item \textbf{Sample and material.} The scatter between samples of the same mixture is large; one simulant and one form of ice (sieved grains mixed with the regolith) are tested, while ice in the lunar regolith may coat or cement the grains; the pores contain air instead of vacuum.
    \item \textbf{Measurement.} The temperature of the sample is not recorded; the scale of the displacement measurement varies from test to test and is controlled by the measured heights.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications for lunar applications}
\label{sec:implications}
% Order-of-magnitude consequences for compaction under foundations, excavation and long-term stability.

The first tests already support some qualitative conclusions for construction on icy regolith; the numbers will follow from the calibrated law.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Icy regolith is not rock.} With as little as about 10\% of ice by mass the mixture keeps compacting under constant load, with a settlement that grows with the logarithm of time or faster. Foundations should be designed for settlement that continues over the service life.
    \item \textbf{Temperature is the main control.} In the polar cold traps (40--100\,K) the creep of ice is extremely slow. The risk comes from the structure itself: a heated habitat warms the ground beneath it towards the temperatures of these tests. Thermal isolation, elevated or ventilated foundations and passive heat extraction, as used on terrestrial permafrost, address this directly. The activation energy measured in the temperature series sets how much warming is acceptable: in the range tested, 10\,K of warming has the same effect on the creep rate as an increase of the load by 40--60\%, while cooling from $-20\,^\circ$C to 100\,K reduces the rate by many orders of magnitude.
    \item \textbf{Removing the ice by heating is not a practical way to stop creep.} Dry regolith creeps very little, but heating icy regolith in vacuum makes the ice sublimate rather than melt. This does not compact the ground: it leaves the volume of the ice as pores, so that the mixture tested here would become a regolith with a void ratio of about 2.6 relative to the mineral grains, prone to large primary compression and collapse under load. The vapour also moves downwards and condenses again in the colder layers below, possibly concentrating ice under the foundation, and the sublimation of the ice in one cubic metre of the mixture requires about 0.7\,GJ. In terrestrial permafrost the corresponding choice is between keeping the ground frozen (passive method) and thawing and consolidating it before construction (active method), the latter used only for ground poor in ice. On the Moon the cold surroundings favour the passive method; if the site must be treated, the ice, which is also a resource, is better extracted and the dry regolith compacted mechanically before construction.
    \item \textbf{Ice content matters most at low values.} The change from dry to about 10\% ice by mass increases $C_\alpha$ by more than an order of magnitude, while further increases have a smaller effect. Mapping the ice content is therefore part of site selection.
    \item \textbf{Extrapolation in stress is the main open question.} Laboratory stresses (2\,MPa) are an order of magnitude above those of a foundation. The stress-step test gives an apparent exponent of about 5--7 between 0.5 and 3.4\,MPa, which would make creep at 0.1--0.2\,MPa much slower than in the tests; the flow law of ice predicts instead a lower exponent at low stress, where grain boundary sliding dominates, and therefore faster creep (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). Until tests at lower stress separate the two, the more conservative extrapolation should be used.
\end{itemize}
